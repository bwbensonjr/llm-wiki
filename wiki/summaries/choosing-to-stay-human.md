# Choosing to Stay Human

## Summary

An essay by [[ethan-mollick|Ethan Mollick]] (*One Useful Thing*) arguing for
intentional rather than reflexive use of AI — making a conscious choice about
which cognitive work to hand over and which to keep. He opens on the flood of
formulaic AI writing online: "meaning-shaped attention vampires" that look
effortful but carry little human meaning (a satirical mock post parodies the
tells — em-dashes, "this is not X, it's Y," nonsense similes). Using AI as a
*default* writing tool, he warns, risks atrophying a skill that takes years of
uncomfortable effort to build.

The core risk is **[[cognitive-surrender|cognitive surrender]]** (a term from his
Wharton colleagues): people stop thinking and defer to the AI even when it is
wrong. He illustrates with education research — a Turkish high-school study where
plain ChatGPT handed students answers and they then underperformed on AI-free
tests (shortcutting effort shortcuts learning), versus a Taipei study where an AI
*tutor* delivering a personalized problem sequence raised final-exam scores ~0.15
SD (roughly six-to-nine months of extra schooling). A BCG experiment (758
consultants, GPT-4) showed AI users excelled on most tasks but did *worse* on one
designed to expose AI error, accepting authoritative-looking wrong answers.

The major chatbots now offer learning/tutor modes that push you to think —
Gemini's Guided Learning, ChatGPT's `/learn`, Claude's "learning" style — though
they're unintuitive to find and only help the willing. Mollick distinguishes
harmless offloading (phone numbers, arithmetic, cursive) from AI's distinctive
danger: it is general enough to absorb almost any cognitive task, and we don't
yet know which skills matter to keep. His closing worry is about **defaults** —
AI firms designing for frictionless use, plus employer and "AI literacy" norms —
habits being set now, largely without planning, that will be hard to reverse.
"The most important thing we can do is keep asking what to hand over and what to
keep for ourselves."

## Why this matters

I think the tendency to defer to AI is an issue to worry about and keep tabs on.
